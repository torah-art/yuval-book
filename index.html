From the Introduction. The Translator from the Language of the Heavens

Yuval, the son of Lemekh, is the first musician in human history. In the genealogies of the early chapters of the Book of Genesis, it is written: “this one lived so many years and begot sons and daughters.” Not so with Yuval. In his case, the biblical writer shares something grand: he became the father of all musicians (Gen 4:21). And he begot… King David! Yes, David ben Yishai (the son of Jesse), who lived three thousand years after Yuval, was his disciple, for he played the harp and flute masterfully.

From the chapter “Let us make man…”

Before we address Adam’s creativity, let us consider how he himself was created, for his creativity is directly dependent upon the Almighty’s design for him.

At the creation of man, the Almighty uttered profound and mysterious words: “And God said, Let us make man in our image TZÉLEM, after our likeness DMUT” (Gen 1:26).

To explain the relationship between image and likeness, let us take the example of a polished diamond and a rough diamond. The polished diamond already exists within the raw stone, just as Michelangelo’s statue of David existed within the block of Carrara marble he selected, though only in potential — it required long hours of work from the master to bring it to life.

From the chapter Yuval — The First Artist

Yuval — the first individual whose art is distinctly characterized by the Torah — is described as the father, meaning the teacher and originator of musical artistry.

Ibn Ezra explains that “father” in this context signifies “the first,” deriving from the same root as the words EV — “the first sprout” — and AVIV — “the first ears of barley.”

YUVÁL — a spring, a stream of water breaking through the earth’s crust. From captivity, the stream bursts forth into freedom, giving rise to a mighty river. Like water directed by its banks, so too are God’s words the banks guiding Yuval’s creativity. Yuval is led by the Almighty.

Who is this book for?

For artists, musicians, designers, and all who simply think creatively. It speaks to both Jewish and Christian readers, especially those interested in reconnecting art with faith, and purpose with practice.

Do I need to know Hebrew?

No. The book is written in English. Hebrew words are given in transliteration with their meaning, so you can follow the reading without knowing the language.

Is this a commentary or a personal reading?

A personal reading. The author works from the Hebrew text of Scripture and cites every verse he uses, but the connections he draws are his own interpretation.
